Q: What is the difference between speed and velocity?
Topic: Kinematics
- Speed is a vector (it has a direction); velocity is just how fast, with no direction
* Velocity is a vector (it has a direction); speed is just how fast, with no direction
- They're the same quantity; physicists say "velocity" and everyone else says "speed"
- Speed is measured over a whole trip, while velocity is measured at a single instant
Why: Running a full lap around a track gives an average velocity of zero (you ended where you started), but your average speed was not zero.

Q: A car starts from rest and accelerates at 3 m/s² for 4 s. How fast is it going?
Topic: Kinematics
- 7 meters per second
* 12 meters per second
- 0.75 meters per second
- 24 meters per second
Why: v = v₀ + at = 0 + 3 × 4 = 12 meters per second. For constant acceleration, the key equations are v = v₀ + at, x = v₀t + ½at², and v² = v₀² + 2ax.

Q: How far does that same car (starting from rest, 3 m/s² for 4 s) travel?
Topic: Kinematics
- 12 meters
* 24 meters
- 48 meters
- 6 meters
Why: x = ½at² = ½ × 3 × 16 = 24 m. Or use the average velocity: (0 + 12)/2 = 6 meters per second, times 4 s, is 24 m.

Q: A ball is dropped from rest. Ignoring air resistance, how far does it fall in 2 seconds?
Topic: Kinematics
- 9.8 meters
* 19.6 meters
- 39.2 meters
- 4.9 meters
Why: y = ½gt² = ½ × 9.8 × 4 = 19.6 m. Distance grows with the square of time, so in 4 seconds it would fall four times as far.

Q: A ball is thrown straight up. At the very top of its path, what are its velocity and acceleration?
Topic: Kinematics
- Both are zero, since the ball has stopped moving for an instant
* Velocity is zero; acceleration is still 9.8 m/s² downward
- Velocity is 9.8 m/s downward; acceleration is zero
- Velocity is zero; acceleration is zero, then becomes 9.8 m/s² as it falls
Why: Gravity never switches off. If the acceleration were zero at the top, the ball would just hang there. This is one of the most-missed conceptual questions in intro physics.

Q: One ball is dropped and another is fired horizontally from the same height at the same moment. Which hits the ground first (no air resistance)?
Topic: Projectile Motion
- The dropped ball
- The fired ball
* They land at the same time
- It depends on the firing speed
Why: Horizontal and vertical motion are independent. Both balls start with zero vertical velocity and fall with the same acceleration, so the horizontal speed doesn't change the fall time.

Q: Ignoring air resistance, which launch angle gives a projectile the longest range on level ground?
Topic: Projectile Motion
- 30°
* 45°
- 60°
- 90°
Why: Range = v² sin(2θ)/g, which is largest when 2θ = 90°. Angles that add up to 90°, like 30° and 60°, give the same range.

Q: On a velocity-versus-time graph, what does the area under the curve represent?
Topic: Kinematics
- Acceleration
* Displacement
- Speed
- Force
Why: Displacement is the integral of velocity. The slope of a velocity graph is acceleration, and the slope of a position graph is velocity. This is calculus at work.

Q: What does Newton's first law say?
Topic: Newton's Laws
- An object's acceleration equals the net force on it divided by its mass
* An object keeps its velocity (at rest, or moving straight at constant speed) unless a net force acts
- An object in motion slowly comes to rest unless a force keeps pushing it forward
- For every force one object exerts on another, there is an equal and opposite force back on it
Why: This is inertia. A hockey puck glides almost forever on ice because there's very little friction. Motion doesn't need a force; changes in motion do.

Q: A net force of 10 N acts on a 2 kg object. What is its acceleration?
Topic: Newton's Laws
- 20 meters per second squared
* 5 meters per second squared
- 0.2 meters per second squared
- 12 meters per second squared
Why: Newton's second law: a = F/m = 10/2 = 5. Use the NET force, the vector sum of all forces, not just one of them.

Q: A truck and a small car collide head-on. How do the forces they exert on each other compare?
Topic: Newton's Laws
- The truck exerts more force, since it has more mass
- The car exerts more force, since it accelerates more
* The forces are equal in size and opposite in direction
- It depends on which vehicle was moving faster
Why: Newton's third law: forces come in pairs acting on DIFFERENT objects. The car accelerates much more, because the same force acts on a smaller mass (a = F/m).

Q: What is the weight of a 50 kg person on Earth?
Topic: Newton's Laws
- 50 newtons
* 490 newtons
- 5 newtons
- 50 kilograms
Why: Weight is a force: W = mg = 50 × 9.8 = 490 N. Mass (in kilograms) is the same everywhere, but weight changes on the Moon.

Q: You stand on a scale in an elevator that's accelerating upward. What does the scale read compared with your normal weight?
Topic: Newton's Laws
* More than your weight
- Less than your weight
- Exactly your weight
- Zero
Why: The scale must push you up with enough force to support you AND accelerate you: N = m(g + a). Accelerating downward makes you feel lighter, and in free fall the scale reads zero.

Q: A 10 kg box slides across a floor with a coefficient of kinetic friction of 0.3. What is the friction force?
Topic: Friction
- 3 newtons
* 29.4 newtons
- 98 newtons
- 32.7 newtons
Why: Kinetic friction = μk × N, and on a flat floor the normal force is N = mg = 98 N, so friction = 0.3 × 98 = 29.4 N. It points opposite the sliding direction.

Q: Why is it harder to start pushing a heavy couch than to keep it moving?
Topic: Friction
- The couch's weight decreases slightly once it starts sliding
* Maximum static friction is usually larger than kinetic friction
- Kinetic friction is usually larger than maximum static friction
- The couch's momentum adds to your push once it's moving
Why: μs is typically greater than μk. That's also why anti-lock brakes help: a rolling tire grips with static friction, which is stronger than a skidding tire's kinetic friction.

Q: A block rests on a ramp tilted at angle θ. What is the component of gravity pulling it down the ramp?
Topic: Forces
- mg cos θ
* mg sin θ
- mg tan θ
- mg
Why: The component pressing into the ramp is mg cos θ, which sets the normal force. At θ = 0 (flat) nothing pulls it along, and at 90° the full weight does, which matches sin θ.

Q: A 5 kg mass hangs at rest from a rope. What is the tension in the rope?
Topic: Forces
- 5 newtons
* 49 newtons
- 0 newtons
- 98 newtons
Why: At rest, the net force is zero, so the tension balances the weight: T = mg = 49 N. A free-body diagram (draw every force on the object) makes problems like this routine.

Q: A car drives around a flat curve at constant speed. What force provides the centripetal force?
Topic: Circular Motion
- The car's engine pushing it toward the center of the curve
* Static friction between the tires and the road
- Kinetic friction from the tires sliding sideways
- The normal force from the road pushing on the car
Why: Turning means the velocity's direction changes, which is acceleration toward the center (v²/r). On ice there's little friction, so the car slides straight. Banked roads let the normal force help.

Q: If a car takes the same curve at twice the speed, how much more centripetal force does it need?
Topic: Circular Motion
- Twice as much
* Four times as much
- √2 times as much
- Eight times as much
Why: F = mv²/r, so the force grows with the square of the speed. That's why speeding on curves is so dangerous.

Q: You carry a heavy box across a room at constant height and constant speed. How much work does your upward holding force do on the box?
Topic: Work & Energy
- A lot, since you must hold up the box's full weight the whole way
* Zero, because the force is perpendicular to the motion
- Your force times the distance across the room
- Negative work, since gravity pulls the box downward
Why: Work = F·d·cos θ, and here θ = 90°, so cos θ = 0. Your muscles get tired, but in the physics sense no work is done on the box.

Q: If you double a car's speed, what happens to its kinetic energy?
Topic: Work & Energy
- It doubles
* It quadruples
- It stays the same
- It halves
Why: KE = ½mv². Four times the energy means about four times the braking distance, another reason speed matters so much.

Q: A ball is dropped from 20 m. Ignoring air resistance, how fast is it moving just before it hits the ground?
Topic: Work & Energy
- 9.8 meters per second
* About 19.8 meters per second
- About 14 meters per second
- About 28 meters per second
Why: Potential energy turns into kinetic energy: mgh = ½mv², so v = √(2gh) = √392 ≈ 19.8. The mass cancels, which is why all objects fall alike without air resistance.

Q: How much gravitational potential energy does a 2 kg book gain when lifted 5 m?
Topic: Work & Energy
- 10 joules
* 98 joules
- 49 joules
- 2.5 joules
Why: U = mgh = 2 × 9.8 × 5 = 98 J. Only the change in height matters, not the path taken.

Q: A motor does 600 J of work in 3 s. What is its power?
Topic: Work & Energy
- 1,800 watts
* 200 watts
- 600 watts
- 3 watts
Why: Power = work ÷ time = 600/3 = 200 W. One watt is one joule per second. Power is the rate of doing work.

Q: What does the work-energy theorem say?
Topic: Work & Energy
- The net work done on an object equals its change in potential energy
* The net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy
- The work done by friction equals the object's total mechanical energy
- The net work done on an object equals its change in momentum
Why: Pushing a cart with a net force over a distance speeds it up by exactly that much kinetic energy. Friction does negative work, which takes kinetic energy away.

Q: Why do airbags reduce injuries in a crash?
Topic: Momentum
- They reduce how much the person's momentum changes during the crash
* They stretch out the stopping time, so the force needed to stop the person is smaller
- They shorten the stopping time, so the person stops before hitting anything
- They absorb the person's momentum, so the car's momentum isn't affected
Why: Impulse = FΔt = Δp. The momentum change is fixed by the crash, so a longer Δt means a smaller F. Helmets, crumple zones, and bending your knees when you land all use the same idea.

Q: A 2 kg cart moving at 3 meters per second hits a 1 kg cart at rest, and they stick together. How fast do they move afterward?
Topic: Momentum
- 3 meters per second
* 2 meters per second
- 1.5 meters per second
- 6 meters per second
Why: Momentum is conserved: 2 × 3 = (2 + 1) × v, so v = 2. In this perfectly inelastic collision, kinetic energy is NOT conserved; some becomes heat and sound.

Q: What is conserved in an elastic collision?
Topic: Momentum
- Only momentum
- Only kinetic energy
* Both momentum and kinetic energy
- Momentum, plus kinetic energy only if the masses are equal
Why: Momentum is conserved in every collision when there are no outside forces. Kinetic energy is conserved only in elastic ones, like billiard balls, approximately.

Q: Why does a longer wrench make it easier to loosen a bolt?
Topic: Rotation
* Torque is force times lever arm (τ = rF sin θ), so a longer arm gives more torque for the same force
- A longer wrench is heavier, so its weight adds extra force to the bolt as you turn it
- A longer wrench lowers the friction in the threads, so the bolt needs less force to turn
- A longer wrench increases the bolt's moment of inertia, so it turns more easily once started
Why: Push at the end of the handle, perpendicular to it (θ = 90°), for the most torque. Doorknobs are placed far from the hinges for the same reason.

Q: A figure skater pulls her arms in while spinning. What happens?
Topic: Rotation
- She slows down, since pulling her arms in takes energy away from her spin
* She spins faster, because her angular momentum stays the same while her moment of inertia drops
- She spins faster, because pulling her arms in adds angular momentum to her spin
- Her spin rate doesn't change, since no outside torque acts on her
Why: Angular momentum L = Iω is conserved with no outside torque. Pulling mass toward the axis lowers I, so ω, the spin rate, rises.

Q: Two wheels have the same mass. One has its mass near the rim and one has it near the center. Which is harder to start spinning?
Topic: Rotation
- The one with mass near the center
* The one with mass near the rim
- They're the same
- It depends on their color
Why: Moment of inertia grows with mass times distance squared from the axis. A hoop has I = MR², while a solid disk has I = ½MR².

Q: If the distance between two masses doubles, what happens to the gravitational force between them?
Topic: Gravity
- It becomes half as strong
* It becomes one quarter as strong
- It becomes twice as strong
- It becomes one eighth as strong
Why: Newton's law of gravitation: F = Gm₁m₂/r², an inverse-square law. Triple the distance and the force becomes one ninth.

Q: Why do astronauts on the International Space Station float?
Topic: Gravity
- There's no gravity at the height of the station, about 400 km above Earth
* They and the station are in free fall together, constantly falling around Earth
- The station spins fast enough that its rotation cancels the pull of gravity
- The Moon's gravity pulls on them just as hard as Earth's, so the forces cancel
Why: At the station's height, gravity is still about 90% as strong as on the ground. They float because everything falls at the same rate, so nothing presses against the floor.

Q: A mass on a spring oscillates with period T. If the mass is made 4 times larger, what is the new period?
Topic: Oscillations
- T/2
- 4T
* 2T
- T
Why: For a spring, T = 2π√(m/k). Four times the mass gives √4 = 2 times the period. A stiffer spring (larger k) oscillates faster.

Q: What does the period of a simple pendulum (with small swings) depend on?
Topic: Oscillations
- Its mass
* Its length (and g)
- How far it's pulled back
- Its color
Why: T = 2π√(L/g). That independence from mass and swing size made pendulums the basis of accurate clocks for centuries.

Q: A satellite moves in a circular orbit at constant speed. Is it accelerating?
Topic: Circular Motion
- No; its speed is constant, so its acceleration must be zero
* Yes, toward Earth's center, because the direction of its velocity keeps changing
- Yes, forward along its path, which keeps it from falling to Earth
- Yes, away from Earth, which exactly balances the pull of gravity
Why: Acceleration is any change in velocity, including direction. Gravity provides the centripetal acceleration that keeps it curving around Earth.

Q: Which quantity is a vector?
Topic: Kinematics
- Mass
- Temperature
* Force
- Energy
Why: Vectors have size and direction, like force, velocity, acceleration and momentum. Scalars have only size, like mass, energy, speed and time.

Q: Using calculus, if position is x(t) = 2t³ meters, what is the acceleration at t = 1 s?
Topic: Kinematics
- 6 meters per second squared
* 12 meters per second squared
- 2 meters per second squared
- 18 meters per second squared
Why: v(t) = dx/dt = 6t², and a(t) = dv/dt = 12t, so a(1) = 12. In calculus-based physics, velocity and acceleration are derivatives of position.

Q: Formula check: which kinematics equation has no time variable in it?
Topic: Formulas
* v² = v₀² + 2aΔx
- v = v₀ + at
- Δx = v₀t + (1/2)at²
- Δx = ((v₀ + v)/2) · t
Why: Use it when a problem gives speeds and distance but no time. All four equations assume constant acceleration.

Q: Formula check: what is the displacement under constant acceleration, starting with velocity v₀?
Topic: Formulas
* Δx = v₀t + (1/2)at²
- Δx = v₀t + at²
- Δx = (1/2)v₀t + at²
- Δx = v₀ + (1/2)at
Why: It's the area under a straight-line velocity graph: a rectangle (v₀t) plus a triangle ((1/2)·t·at). Integrating v = v₀ + at gives the same result.

Q: Formula check: what is Newton's second law?
Topic: Formulas
* F_net = ma
- F_net = mv
- F_net = m/a
- F_net = (1/2)mv²
Why: The net force, not any single force, sets the acceleration. mv is momentum, and (1/2)mv² is kinetic energy.

Q: Formula check: what is the formula for kinetic friction?
Topic: Formulas
* f_k = μ_k · N
- f_k = μ_k · m
- f_k = μ_k / N
- f_k = μ_k · v
Why: N is the normal force, which equals mg only on a flat surface with no other vertical forces. Static friction is f_s ≤ μ_s N, an upper limit, not a fixed value.

Q: Formula check: what is centripetal acceleration for speed v on a circle of radius r?
Topic: Formulas
* a_c = v² / r
- a_c = v / r
- a_c = v r²
- a_c = r / v²
Why: It points toward the center of the circle. The needed net force is F = mv²/r, which is why doubling the speed takes four times the force.

Q: Formula check: what is work done by a constant force F over displacement d at angle θ?
Topic: Formulas
* W = F d cos θ
- W = F d sin θ
- W = F / d
- W = (1/2) F d²
Why: Only the part of the force along the motion does work. At 90° (cos 90° = 0) the work is zero, like carrying a box horizontally.

Q: Formula check: what are kinetic energy and gravitational potential energy near Earth?
Topic: Formulas
* KE = (1/2)mv² and PE = mgh
- KE = mv² and PE = (1/2)mgh
- KE = (1/2)mv and PE = mg/h
- KE = mgh and PE = (1/2)mv²
Why: These two trade back and forth in a falling or swinging object. Setting mgh = (1/2)mv² gives v = √(2gh) for a dropped object.

Q: Formula check: what is impulse?
Topic: Formulas
* J = FΔt = Δp
- J = F / Δt = Δp
- J = FΔx = ΔKE
- J = mΔt = Δv
Why: Impulse equals the change in momentum. Spreading the same Δp over a longer Δt (airbags, bending your knees) means a smaller force.

Q: Formula check: what is torque?
Topic: Formulas
* τ = r F sin θ
- τ = r F cos θ
- τ = F / r
- τ = r² F
Why: r is the distance from the pivot and θ is the angle between r and F. Pushing straight at the hinge (θ = 0) gives no torque, however hard you push.

Q: Formula check: what is Newton's law of universal gravitation?
Topic: Formulas
* F = G m₁m₂ / r²
- F = G m₁m₂ / r
- F = G (m₁ + m₂) / r²
- F = m₁m₂ / (G r²)
Why: It's an inverse-square law: doubling the distance cuts the force to a quarter. G is about 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg².

Q: Formula check: what is the period of a mass m on a spring with spring constant k?
Topic: Formulas
* T = 2π √(m/k)
- T = 2π √(k/m)
- T = 2π √(L/g)
- T = 2π m/k
Why: A heavier mass swings more slowly and a stiffer spring swings faster. 2π√(L/g) is the period of a pendulum, which doesn't depend on the mass at all.

Q: Formula check: what is Hooke's law for a spring?
Topic: Formulas
* F = −kx
- F = kx²
- F = −k/x
- F = (1/2)kx
Why: The spring pulls back in the opposite direction of the stretch, which is the meaning of the minus sign. The energy stored in the spring is (1/2)kx².

Q: Formula check: what is the formula for power?
Topic: Formulas
* P = W / t (or P = F·v)
- P = W · t (or P = F/v)
- P = F / t (or P = m·v)
- P = W / d (or P = F·a)
Why: Power is how fast work is done, measured in watts (joules per second). A motor doing 600 J in 3 s puts out 200 W.
