Q: What does utilitarianism say makes an action right?
Topic: Ethical Theories
- Following your moral duties and rules no matter what consequences they lead to
* Producing the greatest overall good (happiness or well-being) for the greatest number
- Acting the way a person of good character would act in the same situation
- Doing whatever benefits you most in the long run, since everyone acts from self-interest
Why: Bentham and Mill are the classic utilitarians. It focuses on consequences. Critics point out it can seem to justify harming a few people if the total benefit is large enough.

Q: What is the difference between act and rule utilitarianism?
Topic: Ethical Theories
- Rule utilitarianism judges each action by its consequences; act utilitarianism follows the best rules
* Act utilitarianism judges each act by its results; rule utilitarianism follows rules that do best if all follow them
- There's no real difference; "rule" utilitarianism is just the name for Mill's version of the theory
- Act utilitarianism is about obeying the law, while rule utilitarianism is about personal moral codes
Why: Lying might help in one case (act), but a rule of honesty produces better results overall (rule). The rule version tries to avoid some of the troubling conclusions of the act version.

Q: What is Kant's categorical imperative, in its "universal law" form?
Topic: Ethical Theories
- Act in whatever way produces the most happiness for everyone affected by the action
* Act only on principles you could consistently will to become a universal law for everyone
- Act the way you would want others to act toward you, as long as it benefits you too
- Act according to the moral rules your society has agreed on through a social contract
Why: Lying to get a loan fails the test: if everyone lied about repaying, promises would mean nothing. Kant's ethics is deontological, focused on duties and intentions rather than consequences.

Q: Kant's "humanity" formulation says to treat people how?
Topic: Ethical Theories
- As means to achieving the greatest good, as long as their happiness is also counted
* Always as ends in themselves, never merely as a means
- According to how much they contribute to society
- Equally, unless treating someone unequally produces better results
Why: Using people requires their informed consent and respect for their choices. Deceptive "dark patterns" that trick users into buying things arguably treat them merely as a means.

Q: What does virtue ethics focus on?
Topic: Ethical Theories
- The consequences of actions, judged by how much happiness they bring
- Following strict moral rules that apply the same way to every situation
* Character: becoming a good person with virtues like honesty and courage
- The agreements that rational people would make with each other
Why: It comes from Aristotle, who saw virtues as a "golden mean" between extremes: courage lies between cowardice and recklessness. The goal is eudaimonia, a flourishing life.

Q: What does social contract theory claim?
Topic: Ethical Theories
- Morality comes from human nature, so it's the same everywhere regardless of agreements
* Moral and political rules are those rational people would agree to for mutual benefit
- Any agreement people sign is morally binding, whatever it says
- Moral rules come from God and are written into the laws of every society
Why: Hobbes, Locke and Rousseau are key figures. John Rawls's "veil of ignorance" asks what rules you'd choose if you didn't know what position in society you'd end up in.

Q: What is Rawls's veil of ignorance meant to produce?
Topic: Ethical Theories
- Rules that favor whoever is most talented, since talent benefits everyone in society
* Fair principles, since you'd choose them without knowing if you'd be rich or poor, healthy or sick
- Rules that maximize total happiness, even if some people end up much worse off
- Rules that keep society exactly as it is now, since change creates uncertainty
Why: Rawls argued people behind the veil would protect basic liberties and allow inequalities only if they benefit the worst-off (the difference principle).

Q: What is cultural moral relativism?
Topic: Metaethics
- The view that some cultures have better moral codes than others and should spread them
* The view that right and wrong are set by each culture's beliefs, with no universal standard
- The view that morality can be discovered scientifically, like the laws of physics
- The view that each individual decides what's right for themselves, regardless of culture
Why: A common objection: if relativism were true, reformers like abolitionists who opposed their own culture's practices would automatically be wrong. Many philosophers distinguish respecting differences from accepting that anything goes.

Q: In the trolley problem, why do many people who would pull the lever refuse to push a large man off a bridge to stop the trolley, even though both save five?
Topic: Applied Ethics
- Most people miscount and think pushing the man saves fewer lives than pulling the lever
* It suggests how harm is caused matters to us, like using a person as a means vs. redirecting a threat
- Pushing someone is illegal while pulling a lever isn't, and people mainly follow the law
- It shows that most people are strict utilitarians who only care about the number saved
Why: The difference highlights the clash between consequences (5 versus 1 in both cases) and duties or intentions. Self-driving car design has made these puzzles practical.

Q: What makes an argument valid?
Topic: Reasoning
- Its conclusion is actually true
* If its premises were true, the conclusion would have to be true
- Its premises are true and its conclusion follows from them
- Most experts agree with its conclusion
Why: An argument can be valid with false premises: "All cats can fly; Tom is a cat; so Tom can fly." A sound argument is valid AND has true premises.

Q: An app collects users' location data and sells it to advertisers, with that permission buried deep in a long terms-of-service. What is the main ethical problem?
Topic: Privacy
- The data is stored on cloud servers, where it could be lost if the company shuts down
* Meaningful informed consent is missing: most users can't realistically know how their data is used
- Location data has no value to advertisers, so the users are being cheated out of nothing
- Selling any data to advertisers is illegal everywhere, regardless of what the terms say
Why: Privacy ethics stresses transparency, data minimization (collect only what you need) and real consent. Laws like the EU's GDPR and California's CCPA push in this direction.

Q: Why can "anonymized" data still threaten privacy?
Topic: Privacy
- Anonymization deletes most of the data, so what's left is too small to be useful
* Combining it with other data sets can re-identify people; zip code, birth date and sex identify many
- Anonymized data is always stored unencrypted, so anyone who finds it can read the names
- It can't; once names are removed, there is no way to link the data back to individuals
Why: Researchers have re-identified people in "anonymous" movie ratings and medical records. Techniques like differential privacy add mathematical protection.

Q: A company's résumé-screening algorithm, trained on past hires, scores women lower. What's the most important ethical lesson?
Topic: Algorithmic Bias
- Algorithms can't be biased, since they only follow math, so the women's scores must be accurate
* Models can learn and scale up historical discrimination from training data, so they need fairness audits
- The programmers must have intended the bias, since a model only does what it's told to do
- The model should be fixed by removing the gender column, which fully removes any bias
Why: Bias can come from the data, the features chosen, or how "success" is defined. Responsible teams test results across groups and keep humans accountable for decisions.

Q: Why is it hard to make an algorithm "fair"?
Topic: Algorithmic Bias
- Computers can't calculate anything about people, so fairness has to be judged by humans alone
* There are several reasonable fairness definitions (equal accuracy, error rates, selection rates) that often conflict
- Measuring fairness requires collecting sensitive data, which is illegal in every country
- Fairness is easy to achieve with enough data; the problem is only that companies don't bother to try
Why: Choosing a fairness definition is a value judgment, not just a technical decision. This is why ethics is part of the CS curriculum.

Q: According to the ACM Code of Ethics, what should a software engineer do first when asked to ship software they believe could seriously harm the public?
Topic: Professional Responsibility
- Ship it as asked, since the manager is responsible for the decision, not the engineer
* Raise the concern through proper channels, document the risk, and refuse to take part in clearly harmful work
- Secretly change the code so the harmful feature doesn't work, without telling anyone
- Post the code and internal documents online right away so the public can judge the risk for itself
Why: The ACM Code puts the public good first: "avoid harm." Whistleblowing outside the company is sometimes justified, generally after internal channels fail and when the harm is serious and well documented.

Q: The Therac-25 radiation machine in the 1980s gave deadly overdoses. What's the main lesson for software developers?
Topic: Professional Responsibility
- The hardware was faulty, so software engineers had no part in the accidents
* Safety-critical software needs rigorous testing and independent checks; trusting code alone was disastrous
- The operators were poorly trained, so the main fix was better training, not better software
- The software was correct, but the hospitals used the machines in ways they weren't designed for
Why: A race condition and poor error messages led to overdoses and deaths. It's a classic case study in why software engineers bear real ethical responsibility.

Q: What does copyright protect in software?
Topic: Intellectual Property
- The underlying idea or algorithm, so no one else can use the same approach
* The specific expression: the actual code someone wrote
- The program's name and logo, so others can't use them
- The program's features and user interface, but not its code
Why: Ideas aren't copyrightable, but code is. Patents can protect specific inventions (controversially, in software), trademarks protect names and logos, and trade secrets protect confidential information.

Q: What is the core idea of open-source software?
Topic: Intellectual Property
- Software that is free of charge, though its source code stays private
* Source code anyone can read, modify and share under a license granting those rights
- Software with no license at all, so anyone can do anything with it
- Software built by volunteers, which companies aren't allowed to use
Why: Linux, Python and Firefox are open source. Licenses range from permissive (MIT, Apache) to copyleft (GPL, which requires sharing changes under the same terms).

Q: Is "ethical hacking" without the owner's permission ethical?
Topic: Security Ethics
- Yes, as long as your intentions are good and you report what you find afterward
* No; security testing needs authorization, and good intentions don't make unauthorized access okay
- Yes, as long as you don't steal, change or delete any of the data you find
- Only if the system is owned by a large company that can afford to fix the problems
Why: Responsible disclosure and bug bounty programs let researchers report flaws legally. Unauthorized access can break laws like the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act.

Q: If an autonomous car causes a crash, why is responsibility hard to assign?
Topic: AI Ethics
- The car itself can be held legally responsible, since it made the decision that caused the crash
* Many parties are involved (designers, the company, owner, regulators), so blame is hard to pin down
- Nobody is ever responsible, since accidents involving autonomous cars are legally treated as acts of nature
- Only the passenger is responsible, since they chose to ride in the car and could have taken over
Why: Ethicists argue for accountability built into the system: clear testing standards, logging, and assigned responsibility before deployment.

Q: Why is explainability important for AI used in decisions like loans or parole?
Topic: AI Ethics
- Explainable models always run faster, which matters when many decisions must be made quickly
* People affected deserve reasons they can understand and challenge, and errors and bias are easier to catch
- Explainable models are always more accurate than black-box models, so they make better decisions
- It isn't important, as long as the AI's overall accuracy is higher than a human decision-maker's
Why: A "black box" denying someone a loan with no reason undermines fairness and due process. Some laws now give people a right to an explanation or to human review.

Q: What is a "dark pattern" in user interface design?
Topic: Design Ethics
- A dark color scheme that's easier on the eyes at night
* A design that tricks users into doing things they didn't intend, like hidden renewals
- A security feature that hides sensitive information until the user logs in
- A design that uses persuasive wording to encourage healthy habits
Why: Examples include pre-checked boxes, guilt-tripping opt-out buttons ("No thanks, I don't like saving money"), and making cancellation much harder than signing up.

Q: Why is accessibility (designing for users with disabilities) an ethical issue and not just a feature?
Topic: Design Ethics
- It only matters for a small number of users, so it's mostly a question of good customer service
* Shutting people out of essential services like school and banking because of how software is built is unfair, and often illegal
- Accessibility features make apps slower, so the ethical issue is whether the tradeoff is worth it
- It's mainly about choosing pleasant colors and readable fonts, which is a design preference rather than a question of ethics
Why: Screen-reader support, captions, keyboard navigation and good contrast help millions of people, and often help everyone, like captions in a noisy room.

Q: Engagement-optimizing algorithms sometimes spread misinformation. What ethical concern does that raise?
Topic: AI Ethics
- None; if people engage with content, it must be what they want, so promoting it is ethical
* Optimizing only for clicks and watch time can reward sensational or false content, harming public discourse
- Algorithms can't spread content on their own, so only the users who share it are responsible
- Misinformation mainly spreads by word of mouth, so algorithms play no real role in it
Why: What a system optimizes for is an ethical choice. Designers can add other goals, like accuracy signals and friction before sharing, though these involve trade-offs with free expression.

Q: What is the "digital divide"?
Topic: Society & Technology
- A gap in performance between older and newer versions of the same software
* Unequal access to computers, internet and digital skills across incomes, regions and groups
- The difference in how analog and digital devices store and transmit information
- The split between people who prefer using phones and people who prefer computers
Why: During remote school, students without reliable internet fell behind. Technology choices can widen or narrow inequality.

Q: Which ethical framework would most directly ask, "What would an honest, responsible engineer do here?"
Topic: Ethical Theories
- Utilitarianism
- Kantian ethics
* Virtue ethics
- Ethical egoism
Why: Virtue ethics focuses on character and role models. Professional codes often blend all three approaches: consequences (avoid harm), duties (honesty, confidentiality) and virtues (integrity).

Q: What does ethical egoism claim?
Topic: Ethical Theories
- People ought to put others' interests ahead of their own
* People ought to act in their own self-interest
- People always do act in their own self-interest, whether they should or not
- People ought to do what produces the most total happiness
Why: It differs from psychological egoism, the descriptive claim that people DO always act selfishly. Critics argue ethical egoism can't settle conflicts of interest fairly.

Q: Mass surveillance is often defended with "If you have nothing to hide, you have nothing to fear." What's a common reply?
Topic: Privacy
- Everyone has broken some law, so surveillance would eventually catch everyone for something
* Privacy protects free expression, dissent, relationships and dignity, not just secrets, and data power can be abused
- Surveillance doesn't work, since criminals know how to avoid it, so it only catches innocent people
- Hiding information is always wrong, so the real issue is that governments hide their surveillance
Why: People act differently when watched (the "chilling effect"). Data collected for one purpose can later be used for another.

Q: What does the precautionary principle suggest about deploying powerful new technology?
Topic: Society & Technology
- Deploy new technology right away, then fix any problems as they appear
* When an action risks serious or irreversible harm, lack of full certainty isn't a reason to delay safeguards
- Never deploy any new technology until it has been proven to cause no harm at all
- Leave decisions about new technology entirely to the companies that create it and understand it best
Why: It's debated: too much caution can block beneficial innovation. Most AI-safety and environmental discussions weigh this principle against the costs of delay.

Q: Why does it matter who is in the room when technology is designed?
Topic: Society & Technology
- It doesn't matter, since good engineering practices produce good products no matter who designs them
* Diverse teams are more likely to notice how a product might fail or harm people unlike themselves
- Only engineers have the expertise to judge how a product should work, so others aren't needed
- Larger teams make better products, so the number of people matters more than who they are
Why: Examples include soap dispensers whose sensors failed for darker skin and crash-test dummies modeled only on average men. Blind spots become product flaws.

Q: Must-know: which philosopher goes with which ethical theory?
Topic: Must-Know Facts
* Mill: utilitarianism; Kant: deontology; Aristotle: virtue ethics; Hobbes and Rawls: social contract
- Mill: deontology; Kant: utilitarianism; Aristotle: social contract; Rawls: virtue ethics
- Mill: virtue ethics; Kant: social contract; Aristotle: utilitarianism; Hobbes: deontology
- Mill: social contract; Kant: virtue ethics; Aristotle: deontology; Rawls: utilitarianism
Why: Bentham founded utilitarianism and Mill refined it. Kant focused on duty and the categorical imperative, Aristotle on character and the "golden mean," and Rawls on the veil of ignorance.

Q: Must-know: what are the two versions of Kant's categorical imperative?
Topic: Must-Know Facts
* Universal law: act only on rules everyone could follow; humanity: treat people as ends, never merely as means
- Universal law: maximize happiness for all; humanity: act like a virtuous person would
- Universal law: follow the law of your country; humanity: help people who are suffering
- Universal law: do what most people would do; humanity: treat others as they treat you
Why: Lying fails the universal-law test, because if everyone lied, promises would mean nothing. Using someone as a tool without their consent fails the humanity test.

Q: Must-know: what are the three parts of a classic valid argument form called modus ponens?
Topic: Must-Know Facts
* If P then Q; P; therefore Q
- If P then Q; Q; therefore P
- If P then Q; not P; therefore not Q
- P or Q; P; therefore not Q
Why: Modus tollens is also valid: If P then Q; not Q; therefore not P. The second and third options are the fallacies of affirming the consequent and denying the antecedent.

Q: Must-know: what are the 4 principles of biomedical (and much of tech) ethics?
Topic: Must-Know Facts
* Autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice
- Honesty, loyalty, privacy, profit
- Utility, duty, virtue, contract
- Consent, efficiency, transparency, growth
Why: Respect people's choices, do good, avoid harm, and be fair. They're used to reason about AI systems, data use and safety-critical software as well as medicine.

Q: Must-know: what are the core ACM Code of Ethics principles for computing professionals?
Topic: Must-Know Facts
* Contribute to society, avoid harm, be honest, be fair, respect privacy, honor confidentiality
- Maximize profit, obey your manager, protect company secrets, ship on time
- Write clean code, use version control, test everything, document APIs
- Follow the law, avoid lawsuits, protect patents, report competitors
Why: The ACM Code also says the public good should be the central concern, and that professionals should speak up when systems may cause harm.

Q: Must-know: what are the Fair Information Practice Principles (FIPPs) behind most privacy laws?
Topic: Must-Know Facts
* Notice, choice/consent, access, security, data minimization, accountability
- Collect everything, keep it forever, share with partners, sell to advertisers
- Encryption, firewalls, passwords, backups, antivirus, audits
- Speed, accuracy, availability, scalability, redundancy, cost
Why: Laws like the GDPR build on these ideas: tell people what you collect, let them choose, let them see their data, protect it, collect only what you need, and be responsible for it.

Q: Must-know: what is the difference between a sound and a valid argument?
Topic: Must-Know Facts
* Valid: the conclusion follows if the premises are true; sound: valid AND the premises really are true
- Sound: the conclusion follows from the premises; valid: the premises really are true
- They mean the same thing in logic
- Valid means most people accept it; sound means experts accept it
Why: "All cats can fly; Tom is a cat; so Tom can fly" is valid but not sound. Only sound arguments guarantee a true conclusion.

Q: Must-know: what are the three main types of intellectual property protection for software?
Topic: Must-Know Facts
* Copyright (the code), patents (novel inventions), trade secrets (kept confidential)
- Trademarks (the code), copyright (inventions), patents (secrets)
- Licenses, warranties, and terms of service
- Copyright (ideas), patents (names and logos), trademarks (algorithms)
Why: Trademarks protect names and logos, like a product's brand. Copyright protects the expression, not the idea, so two people can write different code for the same algorithm.
